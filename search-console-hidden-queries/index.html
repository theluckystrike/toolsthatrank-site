First party census

Search Console names the search behind a third of your impressions

Filter the Performance report to one of your pages and you expect the list of searches that brought it traffic. On the typical page in our census, Google names the search behind 29.0% of its impressions and withholds the rest. Not because anything is broken. Rare queries are dropped on purpose.

Named on the typical page 29.0% median share of a page's impressions with a query attached
Named across the fleet 35.85% of 1,824,935 impressions on pages that get any query named
Above 200 impressions 7.37% of those pages had no query named at all, so on a real traffic page it is rare

Verified 2026-08-24 against the Search Console searchAnalytics endpoint. Window 2026-05-24 to 2026-08-21, 34 properties.

The measurement

Two gaps, and only one of them matters

Start with the one that sounds dramatic and mostly is not. We pulled the page dimension for every property, then the query and page dimensions together, and asked which pages ever appear with a query beside them. 3,608 of 4,974 never did, which is 72.54% of pages.

That number is far less useful than it looks. Those 3,608 pages hold only 2.96% of all impressions and 2.2% of all clicks. They are a long tail of URLs Google barely showed, not a blind spot over traffic that matters.

The gap worth knowing is the second one. On the 1,366 pages that do get queries named, those query rows sum to 654,152 impressions against 1,824,935 at page level. So even on a page that looks fully reported, roughly 64.15% of its impressions carry no query you can read.

A trap we nearly fell into

Unattributed pages are not worse pages

Pages with no named query earn clicks far less often than pages with one. Raw, it looks like a gulf. But unattributed pages are overwhelmingly small pages, so that comparison is measuring size, not attribution. Match the two groups on impressions and most of the gap disappears.

Why it matters

What this breaks in everyday reporting

Summing query rows to get a page total undercuts it. Across our properties that sum reaches 35.85% of the page level figure, so a report built on query rows alone is reading about a third of the traffic and presenting it as the whole.

It also explains a pattern that wastes a lot of time. A page shows impressions and an empty query list, and the natural conclusion is that something is broken. Below 10 impressions that is simply the normal case.

The rule we follow. Use the page dimension for totals, the query dimension for direction, and never present a query level sum as a complete account of anything.

Method

How we measured this

We measured this on 2026-08-24, over 2026-05-24 to 2026-08-21, against the Google Search Console Search Analytics API on 34 verified properties the operator owns. Two pulls per property, one with the page dimension and one with the query and page dimensions together, both paged through to completion.

Checks that could have broken this

No property came within five rows of a pagination cap, so nothing was truncated. Matching URLs loosely on scheme, www, trailing slash and fragment rescues 32 pages, 0.89%, so the result is not a string matching artefact.

Why the median and not the mean

Withheld impressions are wildly concentrated. One page alone carries 24.4% of the fleet total and the top ten carry 45.5%. The per page median of 29.0% describes a typical page. The fleet figure describes our portfolio mix.

What counts as a named query

The URL appears at least once in the query and page pull. One named query is enough, which is the most generous reading available and still leaves most pages unnamed.

Why two pulls

The gap only appears when the two dimensions are compared. Either pull on its own looks complete.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

Your share will differ from ours. A site living on a few high volume searches loses far less to the threshold than one built on rare long tail phrasing, and our own range of 19.44% to 93.79% shows how wide that spread gets.

We measured what the API returns, not why. Google's stated reason is privacy protection for rare queries. We take that at face value because this data cannot test it.

These are 34 small sites in mixed niches run by one operator, and one property supplies most of the impressions. Every fleet figure here is printed with that property removed as well, so you can see how much depends on it.
